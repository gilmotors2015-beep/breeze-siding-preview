How to measure a house for siding before an estimate.

Good measurements separate each wall, gable, trim area, window detail, and visible repair concern so your siding replacement estimate starts with better context.

Start by measuring each side of the house separately.

For a basic siding estimate conversation, measure the width and height of each elevation: front, back, left, and right. Keep gables, garage returns, porch walls, chimney bump-outs, dormers, and second-story sections separate instead of combining everything into one large number.

That separation matters because siding quantity is only part of the project. Different elevations may have different access, trim, windows, dry rot risk, roofline transitions, deck connections, and wall details. Photos and notes can be just as useful as the square footage.

A homeowner measurement is a starting point. The final siding quantity and scope should be confirmed during an onsite estimate because waste, layout, trim, repairs, and weather details change the real project.

Basic measuring formula

  • For a rectangle, multiply wall width by wall height.
  • For a gable triangle, multiply width by height, then divide by two.
  • Measure small walls and garage returns separately.
  • Note large windows and doors, but do not over-focus on perfect deductions.
  • Add photos of every side so the contractor can see details the numbers miss.

Measure by Elevation

Organized measurements make the estimate conversation cleaner.

Instead of sending one total number, label each side of the home. For example: front elevation, back elevation, left side, right side, garage front, entry return, upper gable, and covered porch wall. This helps connect the measurement to photos and makes it easier to discuss access, material layout, and repair risk.

If you are not comfortable measuring second-story sections, do not climb. Wide photos from the ground are safer and often more useful than a guessed number. A contractor can still use the photos to understand rooflines, gables, windows, and likely scope details before the onsite visit.

Label these areas

  • Front, back, left, and right elevations.
  • Garage walls, gables, dormers, and bump-outs.
  • Deck-adjacent walls and covered entry areas.
  • Walls with visible damage, staining, or soft trim.

What Changes the Quantity

Siding estimates include more than flat wall square footage.

Two homes with the same wall area can need very different siding scopes. Trim package, window count, gable cuts, waste, product profile, access, old siding removal, damaged sheathing, and flashing details all affect the final price and material plan.

For Seattle, Tacoma, Bellevue, Puyallup, and nearby Puget Sound homes, moisture details are especially important. If the old siding failed because of water, shade, low clearances, or poor flashing, the estimate should discuss the cause before new material is installed.

Scope items to note

  • Current siding material if known.
  • Soft trim, swelling, cracks, or peeling paint.
  • Window or door areas with staining or leaks.
  • Decks, patios, stairs, landscaping, or tight access.
  • Whether paint, windows, or trim should be included.

Practical Example

How a rough measurement turns into a siding conversation.

1. Measure walls

A wall that is 32 feet wide and 18 feet tall is about 576 square feet before openings, trim, and waste.

2. Add gables

A gable that is 32 feet wide and 8 feet tall adds about 128 square feet using width times height divided by two.

3. Add context

Photos, damage notes, window count, access, and material goals help turn rough square footage into a realistic scope.

Photos Beat Perfect Math

The best estimate prep combines measurements with clear photos.

Photos help show siding condition, window patterns, rooflines, decks, vegetation, ground clearance, electrical penetrations, vents, and areas where moisture may be trapped. If you can send only one thing before a siding estimate, send wide photos of each side of the home.

Closeups are helpful too, especially for soft trim, cracked boards, swollen siding, water stains, caulk failure, past patches, or places where paint keeps peeling. These clues help identify whether the project is simple replacement, targeted repair, or a larger exterior scope.

Photo checklist

  • One wide photo of each side of the home.
  • Closeups of damaged siding or soft trim.
  • Photos around windows, doors, decks, and roofline transitions.
  • Any known leak, repair, or paint-failure area.
